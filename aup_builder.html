Tool

AI Acceptable Use Policy Builder

Build a syllabus-ready AI policy for one course — assignment by assignment, with a stated reason for every rule.

⏱ 25–40 min 📄 1 policy + 2 supporting docs 🎯 Tool 🗂️ Workshop deliverable

What this makes and when to use it

This builder turns the four policy components from Integrity in the Age of AI into three documents you can use immediately. The syllabus policy is the deliverable — it is the artifact named in the workshop objectives. The disclosure statement is the operational half of it: the form students complete when they submit. The student handout is optional — a plain-language version of the same policy for the week-one conversation, generated from the same answers so it cannot drift out of sync. Use this once per course, before the term starts, and revisit it whenever you redesign an assessment.

There are two policy builders in this module — which one?

Use this one if you are writing a policy for one course and want it set assignment by assignment. It makes you name the skill each assignment assesses, which is what turns a rule into something you can teach and defend.

Use Policy Builder — course & department (Brookdale Community College) if you need a single course-wide statement quickly, or a policy at department or programme level. It also exports Canvas-ready HTML.

They are not alternatives so much as different scopes. A common pattern is to set the department position in the broader tool, then use this one to work out the assignment-by-assignment detail for your own section.

Why this asks for a reason on every line

A blanket “AI is allowed” or “AI is banned” policy fails for the same reason: it never says what the student is supposed to be learning. This builder requires one sentence per assignment naming the skill being assessed, because that sentence is what makes a policy teachable, defensible when a student challenges it, and genuinely useful when you redesign the assignment next term.

On detection software: state your position, don't threaten

This builder will not generate text threatening students with AI detectors — as How AI Detection Works sets out, those tools are unreliable and disproportionately flag multilingual writers, so a policy that rests on them creates more risk than it removes.

But saying nothing is worse than saying the right thing. Students assume the worst when a syllabus is silent, and many institutions run detection at the LMS level whether or not you asked for it — leaving you with no stated position when a flag appears. So Step 4 asks you to declare one. Both options make the same commitment in different circumstances: a flag is not evidence, and on its own it will never affect a grade. That is a stronger statement of this workshop's position than silence, and it is the reciprocal of asking students to disclose their own AI use.

A worked example

One filled-in row, and the policy sentence it produces.

Input

AssignmentLevelSkill being assessed
Literature review 🟡 AI-Assisted (Stoplight: Yellow) Locating, evaluating and synthesising peer-reviewed sources

Generated policy text

“Literature review — AI-Assisted. You may use AI for specified purposes on this assignment, and you must disclose that use. This assignment assesses your ability to locate, evaluate and synthesise peer-reviewed sources, so AI may support your process but may not perform that work for you. Complete the disclosure statement when you submit.”

The value is in the third sentence. It tells the student why the boundary sits where it does, which is the difference between a rule they follow and a rule they resent.
