Definition

Definition

Small pieces of text (roughly ¾ of a word) that AI models process. Concise prompts use fewer tokens and often produce clearer, more focused outputs.

Definition

Including 2–5 input/output examples within your prompt to show the AI the pattern or format you want. More reliable than describing the format in words alone.

Definition

Giving the AI instructions without providing any examples — relying entirely on the model's pre-training. Works for simple tasks; few-shot is better for complex formats.

🔄 Flip for definition

A prompt that creates or improves other prompts — automating the prompt engineering process itself. Used to gather requirements and generate optimized prompts automatically.

🔄 Flip for definition

Rules or limits placed on AI output — word count, format, reading level, required sources — that reduce ambiguity, prevent hallucinations, and improve reliability.

Designing AI outputs so they are usable by all learners — including those with disabilities, ESL learners, and diverse reading levels. Build it into every prompt, not as an afterthought.

Part 1: Introduction to AI and Prompts
Foundations · ~45 Minutes
Analogy: A prompt is like a work order handed to a skilled contractor. The contractor (the AI) is highly capable — but they build exactly what the work order says. A vague work order gets a vague result. A precise, detailed work order gets exactly what you envisioned. The contractor doesn't read your mind; they read your instructions.

What Is a Prompt?

A prompt is the set of instructions given to an AI model. It is the primary way you communicate your intent — and it determines the quality, relevance, and accuracy of everything the AI produces. The difference between a useful AI and a frustrating one is almost always the quality of the prompt.

❌ Weak Prompt

Explain cybersecurity.

What's missing? Audience, depth, examples, format, length, context. The AI can only guess what you want.

✅ Strong Prompt

Explain cybersecurity to first-year college students using simple language and provide three real-world examples of cyber attacks that impacted everyday people.

Audience ✓ Language level ✓ Format (examples) ✓ Count (3) ✓ Relevance ✓

How AI Interprets Your Prompt

AI models don't "think" the way humans do. They analyze your prompt by identifying five key signals — and missing any one of them forces the model to make assumptions. Assumptions are where errors begin.

The 10 Anatomy Components of an Effective Prompt

Click each component to expand the explanation and see a faculty example:

1Purpose or Objective — Tell the AI exactly what you want▾
❌ Vague

Create a lesson plan.

✅ Precise

Create a 50-minute lesson plan on photosynthesis for high school biology students that includes three active learning activities.

2Context — Provide the AI with relevant background information▾

Context is the background the AI doesn't know unless you tell it. Think of it as the briefing you give a colleague before asking for their help.

My students are first-year nursing majors with little prior knowledge of anatomy. They learn best through case studies and clinical examples. This is the second week of the semester.
3Pre-Training Information — Teach the AI before making the request▾

Paste in your rubric, grading criteria, or style guide before making your request. This is especially powerful for consistent, repeatable outputs.

Here is my grading rubric [paste rubric here]. Use it when evaluating student essays. For each criterion, provide a score and a one-sentence justification.
4Relevant Design Details — Specify visual or structural requirements▾
  • Use a two-column table with headers "Strategy" and "Example"
  • Format the response as a numbered list with no sub-bullets
  • Include a visual diagram recommendation for each major concept
  • Present using H2 headers for each section, H3 for sub-sections
5Target Audience — Specify who will read or use the output▾

Level matters

The same topic explained to kindergarteners vs. doctoral students requires completely different vocabulary, depth, and examples. Always specify.

Examples

  • First-year community college students
  • Adult professional learners
  • College faculty (all disciplines)
  • Non-technical business stakeholders
  • Graduate-level researchers
6Tone — Set the register and voice of the output▾

Tone options

  • Professional / Academic
  • Conversational / Friendly
  • Motivational / Encouraging
  • Technical / Precise
  • Neutral / Objective

Why it matters

Without a specified tone, AI defaults to a neutral-professional register. For student-facing materials, a conversational or motivational tone is often more effective.

7Scope and Depth — How much detail do you need?▾
Undefined scope

Explain machine learning.

Could produce 3 sentences or 3,000 words.

Defined scope

Provide a brief overview of machine learning (3–5 sentences) appropriate for a faculty professional development introduction. No technical jargon.

8Format — Specify the output structure▾
  • PowerPoint slide outline (10 slides, 5 bullet points each)
  • HTML webpage with semantic heading structure
  • Multiple-choice quiz (20 questions, 4 answer choices)
  • Excel spreadsheet structure (column headers listed)
  • Infographic content (headline, 5 statistics, call to action)
  • Discussion board prompt (50–75 words, open-ended)
9Specifications — Exact technical requirements▾

Specifications remove the last area of ambiguity. Think of them as the "technical specifications" on a design brief.

  • Exactly 500 words
  • Minimum 3 peer-reviewed citations from the last 5 years
  • APA 7th edition format throughout
  • Reading level: Grade 10 (Flesch-Kincaid)
  • Output as clean HTML, no inline styles
10Open-Ended vs. Binary — Choose the right question type▾

✅ Open-Ended

Generates analysis, explanation, and nuance.

Example: "Explain the causes of inflation and how they interact over time."

⚠️ Binary

Forces a Yes/No answer — useful for fact-checking, not exploration.

Example: "Is inflation currently above 3%? Answer Yes or No."

Binary prompts are prone to hallucinations when the AI is uncertain.

Part 2: Building Better Prompts
Frameworks · ~45 Minutes
Analogy: The O-C-A-F framework is like a recipe card. A recipe without ingredients (context) or serving size (audience) is incomplete. You might produce something edible — but not what you intended. O-C-A-F gives every prompt its essential ingredients.

The O-C-A-F Framework

The simplest and most practical prompt structure for faculty. Four components that cover the core requirements of most instructional and administrative prompts.

O — Objective

What do you want the AI to produce? Use a clear action verb: Create, Summarize, Explain, Analyze, Compare, Design.

C — Context

What background does the AI need to produce a relevant response? Course level, topic, prior student knowledge, institutional constraints.

A — Audience

Who will use or read this output? First-year students, graduate researchers, academic administrators, working professionals.

F — Format

How should the output be structured? Table, numbered list, slide outline, quiz, narrative paragraph, HTML, bullet points.

O-C-A-F in Practice

Create [OBJECTIVE] a lesson plan about climate change [CONTEXT] for first-year college students [AUDIENCE] in table format [FORMAT].

This four-component structure produces a more relevant, usable output than any one component alone. Add specifications (Part 1, Component 9) to push it further.

Progressive Prompt Refinement

One of the most powerful skills in prompt engineering is iterative improvement. Click each stage to see how a simple prompt evolves into a fully-formed, high-quality instruction:

1Draft Prompt — Basic intent, no context▾
Starting point

Create a lesson on climate change.

What's missing: Duration, audience, depth, format, activities, learning objectives. The AI will produce something generic and probably unusable as-is.

2Add Duration — Better, but still missing audience and structure▾
Create a one-hour lesson on climate change.

Improvement: Duration added. Still missing: Who is this for? What level? What format? What should students be able to do afterward?

3Add Audience + Pedagogy — Much more targeted▾
Create a one-hour lesson on climate change for introductory environmental science students. Include active learning activities that require students to analyze data, not just listen to lecture.

Improvement: Audience, activity type, and pedagogical approach specified. Still missing: Format, learning objectives, assessment.

4Full O-C-A-F + Specifications — Ready to use▾
✅ Expert Prompt

Create a one-hour lesson plan on climate change for introductory environmental science students at a community college. Include: (1) three measurable learning objectives using Bloom's Taxonomy action verbs, (2) a 10-minute warm-up activity using real climate data, (3) a 30-minute small-group analysis activity, (4) a 10-minute class debrief, and (5) a formative exit ticket. Present the entire lesson in a table format with columns for: Time | Activity | Instructions | Materials. Tone: academic but accessible.

Part 3: Accuracy, Constraints & Token Efficiency
Reliability · ~60 Minutes
Analogy: Hallucinations are like a confident student who hasn't studied — they give you a fluent, well-structured answer that sounds authoritative but contains significant factual errors. The antidote isn't distrust; it's verification. Research shows strategic prompt engineering can reduce hallucination rates by up to 36%.

Understanding and Reducing Hallucinations

AI hallucinations occur when the model generates plausible-sounding but incorrect, fabricated, or misleading information. Studies suggest chatbots fabricate facts approximately 27% of the time, with up to 46% of generated texts containing factual errors when prompts lack sufficient constraints.

🔗 Ask for Sources

Force the AI to cite its claims

🔄 Click
Anti-Hallucination

Add: "Cite peer-reviewed sources from the last 5 years." If it can't cite, it shouldn't claim. Requiring sources discourages invention.

🔍 Require Verification

🔄 Click
Anti-Hallucination

Add: "Identify any assumptions you are making" or "If you are uncertain about any fact, say so explicitly." Forces the model to acknowledge gaps rather than fill them with guesses.

🧠 Chain-of-Thought

🔄 Click
Anti-Hallucination

Simply adding "Think step by step" or "Show your reasoning" forces the AI through logical steps before concluding. Research shows this improves accuracy by 30–35% on reasoning tasks.

Paste in source material

🔄 Click
Anti-Hallucination

Paste your own readings, data, or documents and add: "Answer using only the text I provided. Do not use external knowledge." This is the most powerful anti-hallucination technique available.

Anti-Hallucination

Follow up with: "Review your answer and identify any claims you are not fully certain about." This meta-cognitive step surfaces errors the initial response missed.

What are significant research findings on active learning in higher education? Cite real peer-reviewed sources from the last 10 years. If you are not certain a source is real and accurate, do not include it. Flag any claims where your confidence is low.

Writing Effective Constraints

Constraints are guardrails that reduce ambiguity and produce more consistent, accurate outputs. Think of them as the "quality control checklist" on every prompt.

📏 Length Constraints

  • Maximum 300 words
  • Exactly 5 bullet points per section
  • No more than 2 paragraphs per concept

📚 Style Constraints

  • Use APA 7th edition format
  • No passive voice
  • No technical jargon above Grade 10 level

🎯 Scope Constraints

  • Focus only on the United States context
  • Cover only the period 1945–1990
  • Use only the examples I provide

⚠️ Exclusion Constraints

  • Do not include information about X
  • Avoid mentioning competitor products
  • Do not speculate — only use verified facts

Token Efficiency: Say More with Less

Every word in your prompt consumes tokens (roughly ¾ word each). More tokens = more processing time and sometimes worse results, as the model may lose track of key instructions buried in verbose prompts. The goal is precision, not brevity — but eliminate every unnecessary word.

❌ Token-Inefficient

I need a quiz and it should probably have around ten questions and maybe multiple choice and make it suitable for students who are learning biology for the first time and I would like it to cover photosynthesis if possible...

~55 words. Vague ("probably," "maybe," "if possible"). Conversational filler wastes tokens and weakens instructions.

✅ Token-Efficient

Create a 10-question multiple-choice quiz on photosynthesis for introductory biology students. Four answer choices per question. Include an answer key.

~25 words. Specific, direct, unambiguous. Every word carries meaning.

Designing for Accessibility

Accessible prompts produce outputs that work for all learners — not just those without disabilities. Accessibility is not optional; it's standard practice in higher education.

  • Plain language: "Write at a Grade 8 reading level (Flesch-Kincaid). Define any technical terms used."
  • Alt text: "For each image or diagram referenced, write a one-sentence image description suitable for a screen reader."
  • Multiple reading levels: "Create two versions: one for typical college students and one for ESL learners at intermediate English proficiency."
  • Color accessibility: "Design using high-contrast color combinations. List specific hex codes. Avoid red/green combinations."
  • Screen reader compatibility: "Use semantic HTML headers (H1, H2, H3). Avoid tables for layout; use them only for data."

Reusable Prompt Templates

The most time-efficient faculty use brackets [ ] as placeholders — creating templates that can be filled in for any course, topic, or student group.

Create a [TYPE OF ASSIGNMENT] about [TOPIC] for [AUDIENCE] at [INSTITUTION TYPE]. Include [SPECIFIC COMPONENTS]. Present in [FORMAT]. Tone: [TONE]. Length: [SPECIFICATIONS].

Few-Shot Prompting: Teaching by Example

I want you to classify each item as a specific animal category. Here are some examples: Input: Dog → Output: Mammal Input: Eagle → Output: Bird Input: Salmon → Output: Fish Input: Python → Output: Reptile Now classify the following: Input: Shark → ? Input: Bat → ? Input: Penguin → ?

The AI learns the pattern from the examples — no lengthy description of classification rules needed. This works for any repeatable format: quiz questions, email responses, lesson plan structures.

Chain Prompting: Building Complex Outputs in Stages

Rather than asking for a complete course module in one prompt, chain the task into sequential steps where each output feeds the next:

1️⃣

Create Objectives

"Write 3 measurable learning outcomes for a lesson on [topic] using Bloom's action verbs."

2️⃣

Design Activities

"Using these objectives: [paste], create 3 active learning activities aligned to each objective."

3️⃣

Create Assessments

"Using these objectives and activities: [paste], create a formative and summative assessment."

4️⃣

Build the Plan

"Combine all of the above into a complete 60-minute lesson plan in table format."

Part 5: Meta Prompts & Prompt-Building Tools
Automation & Tools · ~60 Minutes
Analogy: A meta prompt is like hiring a translator who first asks you the right questions before translating your message. Instead of giving the AI your half-formed idea, the meta prompt extracts all the necessary information from you — then builds the fully-specified prompt automatically.

What Is a Meta Prompt?

A meta prompt is a prompt that creates or improves other prompts. Rather than trying to write a perfect prompt immediately, you use a meta prompt to engage the AI in a structured dialogue that gathers all necessary information and then produces an optimized prompt for you.

Meta prompts are especially valuable when you know what you need to accomplish but aren't sure how to express it precisely enough for effective AI output.

Three Ready-to-Use Meta Prompts

1Universal Meta Prompt — Works for any task▾
Act as an expert prompt engineer. Ask me questions one at a time about my task, audience, desired format, tone, and goals. After gathering all necessary information, create a fully optimized AI prompt I can use immediately. Ask only one question at a time and wait for my answer before proceeding.

This is the most versatile meta prompt available. The AI interviews you, then writes the perfect prompt based on your answers.

2Educational Meta Prompt — For lesson and curriculum design▾
Create a detailed, fully-specified prompt that will generate a complete lesson plan for a college course. The prompt should instruct the AI to include: measurable learning objectives using Bloom's Taxonomy action verbs, at least two active learning activities, a formative assessment, an exit ticket, UDL accommodations, and a materials list. Format: table.

Use this once — then use the generated prompt every time you need a new lesson plan. Fill in [TOPIC] and [AUDIENCE] each time.

3Research Meta Prompt — For literature reviews and synthesis▾
Create a prompt that will guide a faculty researcher through an efficient literature review on a topic of their choice. The prompt should instruct the AI to: (1) identify the 5 most relevant subtopics, (2) summarize the scholarly consensus on each, (3) flag areas of ongoing debate, (4) identify methodological trends, and (5) suggest 5 search terms for database research. Include a warning to verify all sources independently.

The PROMPT Framework for Prompt Design

A systematic six-component framework for building professional-grade prompts in any discipline:

P

Purpose

What exactly do you want? Be specific with action verbs. Vague goals produce vague outputs.

R

Role

Who should the AI be? Assign a relevant professional role or persona to focus tone and expertise.

O

Output

What format should the result take? Table, quiz, narrative, HTML, slide deck, rubric.

M

Method

What steps or approach should the AI follow? Chain prompting, think step by step, few-shot examples.

P

Parameters

Constraints: word count, reading level, citation style, tone, exclusions, accessibility requirements.

T

Testing

Review the output. Refine the prompt. Run again. Prompt engineering is iterative — the first output is a draft.

Key Takeaways

  • A prompt is a work order — the more specific and complete, the better the result
  • O-C-A-F (Objective · Context · Audience · Format) is the foundation of every effective prompt
  • Constraints reduce hallucinations — always specify what to include, exclude, and verify
  • Persona + chain-of-thought + few-shot examples are the three most powerful technique combinations
  • Meta prompts automate the prompt creation process itself — saving time for complex recurring tasks
  • Prompt engineering is iterative — the first output is a draft, not a final product
  • Reusable templates with [ ] placeholders are your most valuable long-term productivity asset
