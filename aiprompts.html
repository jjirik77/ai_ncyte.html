AI Prompts & Prompt Engineering

The quality of your AI output is only as good as the quality of your prompt. This workshop teaches you to design, refine, and optimize prompts that produce accurate, reusable, and accessible results for teaching, research, and administration.

⏱ 4–6 hours 📑 5 parts + capstone 🎯 Faculty Workshop 📚 13 key terms Reviewed August 2026

Why this matters

Faculty rarely abandon AI because the model is weak. They abandon it because the first output was generic, and nothing in the interface explained why. A prompt is a work order: the same model that produced the generic answer will produce a usable one when the order says who it is for, what shape it takes, and what it must not do.

Learning objectives

By the end of this workshop you will be able to:

  1. Design prompts that supply the five signals a generative AI model reads: context, instructions, desired output, constraints, and prior conversation
  2. Construct a prompt from the ten anatomy components of an effective instruction
  3. Design prompts that improve accuracy, relevance, and consistency using the CO-STAR framework
  4. Apply prompt engineering techniques that reduce hallucinations and improve output quality
  5. Produce advanced prompts using personas, few-shot examples, chain prompting, and meta prompts
  6. Create reusable prompt templates for teaching, assessment, research, and administrative tasks

Part 1: Introduction to AI and Prompts

Foundations · ~45 Minutes

Analogy: A prompt is like a work order handed to a skilled contractor. The contractor (the AI) is highly capable — but they build exactly what the work order says. A vague work order gets a vague result. A precise, detailed work order gets exactly what you envisioned. The contractor doesn't read your mind; they read your instructions.

What Is a Prompt?

A prompt is the set of instructions given to an AI model. It is the primary way you communicate your intent — and it determines the quality, relevance, and accuracy of everything the AI produces. The difference between a useful AI and a frustrating one is almost always the quality of the prompt.

❌ Weak Prompt

Explain cybersecurity.

What's missing? Audience, depth, examples, format, length, context. The AI can only guess what you want.

✅ Strong Prompt

Explain cybersecurity to first-year college students using simple language and provide three real-world examples of cyber attacks that impacted everyday people.

Audience ✓ Language level ✓ Format (examples) ✓ Count (3) ✓ Relevance ✓

How AI Interprets Your Prompt

AI models don't "think" the way humans do. They analyze your prompt by identifying five key signals — and missing any one of them forces the model to make assumptions. Assumptions are where errors begin.

Part 2: Building Better Prompts

Frameworks · ~45 Minutes

Analogy: The CO-STAR framework is like a recipe card. A recipe without ingredients (context) or serving size (audience) is incomplete. You might produce something edible — but not what you intended. CO-STAR gives every prompt its essential ingredients, and names each one so you can tell at a glance which is missing.

The CO-STAR Framework

Part 1 gave you ten anatomy components — a complete checklist, but too long to hold in your head while you type. CO-STAR is that checklist compressed into six named slots you can run through in about fifteen seconds. It is the framework this workshop uses from here to the capstone, and the one Module 3 is built around.

C

Context

What situation is this for? The course, the point in the term, the prior knowledge students bring, the institutional constraints you are working inside.

"This is week two of an introductory environmental science course at a community college; students have no statistics background."

O

Objective

What do you want the AI to produce, and what must it accomplish? Lead with a clear action verb: Create, Summarize, Explain, Analyze, Compare, Design.

"Create a one-hour lesson plan that gets students analyzing real climate data rather than listening to a lecture."

S

Style

What kind of writing is this? Name the genre or the expertise behind it — textbook chapter, lab protocol, case narrative, Socratic dialogue — or assign a persona (Part 4) to supply it.

"Write it the way an experienced lab instructor writes a hands-on protocol: numbered steps, no preamble."

T

Tone

What attitude should the voice carry? Academic, conversational, motivational, technical, neutral. Left unstated, the model defaults to neutral-professional (Part 1, Component 6).

"Academic but accessible — encouraging, never condescending."

A

Audience

Who will read or use the output? This drives vocabulary, depth, and which examples land. First-year students, graduate researchers, administrators, working professionals.

"First-year community college students, most of them taking their first science course since high school."

R

Response

What shape should the output take, and what must it satisfy? Table, numbered list, slide outline, quiz, HTML — plus the length, citation style, and accessibility requirements it must meet.

"A four-column table: Time | Activity | Instructions | Materials. Under 600 words. Include alt text for any diagram you reference."

Style and Tone are not the same control

This is the distinction faculty most often collapse. Style is the kind of document — a lab protocol, a case narrative, a textbook chapter. Tone is the attitude in the voice — clinical, warm, urgent. A lab protocol can be written warmly; a case narrative can be written coldly. Specify both and you control the register of the output; specify neither and the model picks a bland middle for you. You have already met both halves: Style is what Part 3 calls a style constraint and what Part 4's persona technique buys you; Tone is Component 6 of the prompt anatomy.

CO-STAR in Practice

[CONTEXT] Week two of an introductory environmental science course at a community college. [OBJECTIVE] Create a one-hour lesson plan on climate change. [STYLE] Write as an experienced science instructor building a hands-on session, not a lecture. [TONE] Academic but accessible. [AUDIENCE] First-year college students with no statistics background. [RESPONSE] A table with columns Time | Activity | Instructions | Materials. Under 600 words.

The labels are scaffolding, not syntax — the model reads plain prose just as well. Write it in brackets while you are learning the six slots, then drop them once you stop forgetting any. What matters is that all six are present.

Related frameworks

You will meet other acronyms in the wild. The workshop hub lists CRAFT (Context · Role · Action · Format · Tone) alongside CO-STAR as a Module 3 topic, and prior cohorts of Module 6 were taught RCODC (Role · Context · Objectives · Deliverable · Constraints). Neither is a competing theory. They are the same handful of ideas with different letters over them:

Their componentFrameworkWhere it lives in CO-STAR
ContextCRAFT · RCODCContext — identical
RoleCRAFT · RCODCStyle when it is a persona ("act as a nursing educator"); Context when it is really situational background
ActionCRAFTObjective — the verb and the thing to produce
FormatCRAFTResponse
ToneCRAFTTone — identical
ObjectivesRCODCObjective, stated as the learning outcome the output must serve
DeliverableRCODCResponse — the artifact and its shape
ConstraintsRCODCResponse for limits on the artifact (length, reading level, accessibility); Objective for limits on scope ("nothing requiring clinical placement")

CO-STAR is the superset: everything CRAFT and RCODC name has a home in it, and it adds the one component neither states outright — Audience. CRAFT and RCODC both bury the reader inside Context, which is exactly how prompts end up written at the wrong level.

Progressive Prompt Refinement

One of the most powerful skills in prompt engineering is iterative improvement. Click each stage to see how a simple prompt evolves into a fully-formed, high-quality instruction:

Starting point

Create a lesson on climate change.

One CO-STAR component of six. Objective is present but thin; Context, Style, Tone, Audience, and Response are all absent. The AI will produce something generic and probably unusable as-is.

Create a one-hour lesson on climate change.

Improvement: the Objective now carries a duration, so the AI knows the scale of what it is building. Still missing: Context, Style, Tone, Audience, Response — who is this for, at what level, in what shape, and what should students be able to do afterward?

Create a one-hour lesson on climate change for introductory environmental science students. Include active learning activities that require students to analyze data, not just listen to lecture.

Improvement: Audience is now stated, and the pedagogical approach begins to imply a Style. Still missing: Response — no format, no measurable objectives, no assessment — and Tone is still unset.

✅ Expert Prompt

Create a one-hour lesson plan on climate change for introductory environmental science students at a community college. Include: (1) three measurable learning objectives using Bloom's Taxonomy action verbs, (2) a 10-minute warm-up activity using real climate data, (3) a 30-minute small-group analysis activity, (4) a 10-minute class debrief, and (5) a formative exit ticket. Write it the way an experienced instructor drafts a hands-on session plan rather than a lecture outline. Present the entire lesson in a table format with columns for: Time | Activity | Instructions | Materials. Tone: academic but accessible.

All six now present: Context (introductory course, community college) · Objective (one-hour lesson plan, five named parts) · Style (hands-on session plan, not a lecture outline) · Tone (academic but accessible) · Audience (introductory environmental science students) · Response (four-column table).

Part 3: Accuracy, Constraints & Token Efficiency

Reliability · ~60 Minutes

Analogy: Hallucinations are like a confident student who hasn't studied — they give you a fluent, well-structured answer that sounds authoritative but contains significant factual errors. The antidote isn't distrust; it's verification. Research shows strategic prompt engineering can reduce hallucination rates by up to 36%.

Understanding and Reducing Hallucinations

AI hallucinations occur when the model generates plausible-sounding but incorrect, fabricated, or misleading information. Studies suggest chatbots fabricate facts approximately 27% of the time, with up to 46% of generated texts containing factual errors when prompts lack sufficient constraints.

🔍 Activity 3 · Hallucination Hunt

Compare: Basic Prompt vs. Constrained Prompt

Run both prompts below in any AI tool. Compare the outputs for factual accuracy, source quality, and confidence language.

❌

Basic Prompt (hallucination-prone)

What are the five most significant studies on active learning in higher education?

⚠️ The AI may invent study titles, author names, and publication dates with total confidence.

✅

Constrained Prompt (hallucination-resistant)

What are significant research findings on active learning in higher education? Cite real peer-reviewed sources from the last 10 years. If you are not certain a source is real and accurate, do not include it. Flag any claims where your confidence is low.

Writing Effective Constraints

Constraints are guardrails that reduce ambiguity and produce more consistent, accurate outputs. Think of them as the "quality control checklist" on every prompt.

📏

Length Constraints

  • Maximum 300 words
  • Exactly 5 bullet points per section
  • No more than 2 paragraphs per concept
📚

Style Constraints

  • Use APA 7th edition format
  • No passive voice
  • No technical jargon above Grade 10 level
🎯

Scope Constraints

  • Focus only on the United States context
  • Cover only the period 1945–1990
  • Use only the examples I provide
⚠️

Exclusion Constraints

  • Do not include information about X
  • Avoid mentioning competitor products
  • Do not speculate — only use verified facts

Token Efficiency: Say More with Less

Every word in your prompt consumes tokens (roughly ¾ word each). More tokens = more processing time and sometimes worse results, as the model may lose track of key instructions buried in verbose prompts. The goal is precision, not brevity — but eliminate every unnecessary word.

❌ Token-Inefficient

I need a quiz and it should probably have around ten questions and maybe multiple choice and make it suitable for students who are learning biology for the first time and I would like it to cover photosynthesis if possible...

~55 words. Vague ("probably," "maybe," "if possible"). Conversational filler wastes tokens and weakens instructions.

✅ Token-Efficient

Create a 10-question multiple-choice quiz on photosynthesis for introductory biology students. Four answer choices per question. Include an answer key.

~25 words. Specific, direct, unambiguous. Every word carries meaning.

Designing for Accessibility

Accessible prompts produce outputs that work for all learners — not just those without disabilities. Accessibility is not optional; it's standard practice in higher education.

  • Plain language: "Write at a Grade 8 reading level (Flesch-Kincaid). Define any technical terms used."
  • Alt text: "For each image or diagram referenced, write a one-sentence image description suitable for a screen reader."
  • Multiple reading levels: "Create two versions: one for typical college students and one for ESL learners at intermediate English proficiency."
  • Color accessibility: "Design using high-contrast color combinations. List specific hex codes. Avoid red/green combinations."
  • Screen reader compatibility: "Use semantic HTML headers (H1, H2, H3). Avoid tables for layout; use them only for data."

Reusable Prompt Templates

The most time-efficient faculty use brackets [ ] as placeholders — creating templates that can be filled in for any course, topic, or student group.

Create a [TYPE OF ASSIGNMENT] about [TOPIC] for [AUDIENCE] at [INSTITUTION TYPE]. Include [SPECIFIC COMPONENTS]. Present in [FORMAT]. Tone: [TONE]. Length: [SPECIFICATIONS].

Few-Shot Prompting: Teaching by Example

I want you to classify each item as a specific animal category. Here are some examples: Input: Dog → Output: Mammal Input: Eagle → Output: Bird Input: Salmon → Output: Fish Input: Python → Output: Reptile Now classify the following: Input: Shark → ? Input: Bat → ? Input: Penguin → ?

The AI learns the pattern from the examples — no lengthy description of classification rules needed. This works for any repeatable format: quiz questions, email responses, lesson plan structures.

Chain Prompting: Building Complex Outputs in Stages

Rather than asking for a complete course module in one prompt, chain the task into sequential steps where each output feeds the next:

1️⃣

Create Objectives

"Write 3 measurable learning outcomes for a lesson on [topic] using Bloom's action verbs."

2️⃣

Design Activities

"Using these objectives: [paste], create 3 active learning activities aligned to each objective."

3️⃣

Create Assessments

"Using these objectives and activities: [paste], create a formative and summative assessment."

4️⃣

Build the Plan

"Combine all of the above into a complete 60-minute lesson plan in table format."

Part 5: Meta Prompts & Prompt-Building Tools

Automation & Tools · ~60 Minutes

Analogy: A meta prompt is like hiring a translator who first asks you the right questions before translating your message. Instead of giving the AI your half-formed idea, the meta prompt extracts all the necessary information from you — then builds the fully-specified prompt automatically.

What Is a Meta Prompt?

A meta prompt is a prompt that creates or improves other prompts. Rather than trying to write a perfect prompt immediately, you use a meta prompt to engage the AI in a structured dialogue that gathers all necessary information and then produces an optimized prompt for you.

Meta prompts are especially valuable when you know what you need to accomplish but aren't sure how to express it precisely enough for effective AI output.

Three Ready-to-Use Meta Prompts

Act as an expert prompt engineer. Ask me questions one at a time about my task, audience, desired format, tone, and goals. After gathering all necessary information, create a fully optimized AI prompt I can use immediately. Ask only one question at a time and wait for my answer before proceeding.

This is the most versatile meta prompt available. The AI interviews you, then writes the perfect prompt based on your answers.

Create a detailed, fully-specified prompt that will generate a complete lesson plan for a college course. The prompt should instruct the AI to include: measurable learning objectives using Bloom's Taxonomy action verbs, at least two active learning activities, a formative assessment, an exit ticket, UDL accommodations, and a materials list. Format: table.

Use this once — then use the generated prompt every time you need a new lesson plan. Fill in [TOPIC] and [AUDIENCE] each time.

Create a prompt that will guide a faculty researcher through an efficient literature review on a topic of their choice. The prompt should instruct the AI to: (1) identify the 5 most relevant subtopics, (2) summarize the scholarly consensus on each, (3) flag areas of ongoing debate, (4) identify methodological trends, and (5) suggest 5 search terms for database research. Include a warning to verify all sources independently.

The PROMPT Model: CO-STAR Extended for Meta-Prompt Work

A meta prompt has a harder job than an ordinary prompt: it has to specify not only what the output should be, but how the AI should go about producing it and how you will know it worked. The PROMPT model is CO-STAR with those two extra jobs added — a specialised extension for building prompt-writing prompts, not a competing framework.

P

Purpose

What exactly do you want? Be specific with action verbs. Vague goals produce vague outputs.

CO-STAR: Objective.

R

Role

Who should the AI be? Assign a relevant professional role or persona to focus expertise.

CO-STAR: Style — the persona form of it.

O

Output

What format should the result take? Table, quiz, narrative, HTML, slide deck, rubric.

CO-STAR: Response.

M

Method

What steps or approach should the AI follow? Chain prompting, think step by step, few-shot examples.

New here — CO-STAR describes the destination, not the route.

P

Parameters

Constraints: word count, reading level, citation style, tone, exclusions, accessibility requirements.

CO-STAR: Response requirements, plus Tone.

T

Testing

Review the output. Refine the prompt. Run again. Prompt engineering is iterative — the first output is a draft.

New here — the discipline that makes a reusable prompt worth reusing.

How the two fit together

Four of PROMPT's six components are CO-STAR components under different names. The two that are genuinely new — Method and Testing — are exactly what a meta prompt needs and an ordinary prompt does not: the AI is being asked to run a process (interview you, then draft) and you are being asked to iterate on the result. Notice also what PROMPT drops: it never names Audience. That is safe here only because the meta prompt's job is to ask you who the audience is. Use CO-STAR to write prompts; reach for PROMPT when you are writing a prompt whose output is another prompt.

Transfer to your classroom

The capstone is a contrast case, not a worksheet. Take one real teaching task from this semester, write it four ways — Basic (Objective only), Improved (full CO-STAR), Expert (CO-STAR deepened with persona, constraints, and chain-of-thought), and Meta — run all four in the same AI assistant, and rate each output. The gap between version one and version four is the whole argument of this workshop, and you can only see it on your own material.

Your Faculty Prompt Library from Part 5 is the reusable half of that work: the lesson-plan, rubric, and quiz templates you write there are the prompts that generate the Lesson Plan, Study Guide, and Quiz / Assessment submission artifacts for this workshop.

Key takeaways

  • A prompt is a work order — the more specific and complete, the better the result. The model reads your instructions, not your mind.
  • The ten anatomy components are the checklist behind every good prompt: objective, context, pre-training information, design details, audience, tone, scope, format, specifications, and question type.
  • CO-STAR (Context · Objective · Style · Tone · Audience · Response) is the foundation of every effective prompt, and the superset of the other prompt acronyms in circulation, while prompt engineering remains iterative: the first output is a draft, not a final product.
  • Constraints reduce hallucinations — always specify what to include, what to exclude, and what to verify.
  • Persona, chain-of-thought, and few-shot examples are the three most powerful technique combinations; meta prompts automate the prompt-writing itself for complex recurring tasks.
  • Reusable templates with [ ] placeholders are your most valuable long-term productivity asset.
