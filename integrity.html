Section 01
Why Academic Integrity Is Under Pressure

Generative AI has fundamentally altered the academic landscape. Since ChatGPT's public release, higher education institutions worldwide are grappling with how to maintain academic integrity while embracing the potential of AI technologies.

The Current Reality
  • AI tools are already widely used by students across all disciplines
  • Less than half of U.S. states have official AI policies
  • Faculty report mixed feelings about AI integration
  • Traditional assessment methods face new challenges
💡 Key Insight

"The future is now. We need to be at the forefront of how this technology is evaluated and used." — NEA Secretary-Treasurer Noel Candelaria. Simply waiting or trying to prevent AI use will not serve our students. The question is not whether AI will be used, but how we can guide its ethical and educational use.

The Paradigm Shift
  • From prohibition to integration
  • From detection to authentic assessment
  • From individual policies to institutional frameworks
  • From fear to strategic adoption
Section 02
The Four Major Concerns
🎯
Academic Dishonesty
  • Students submitting AI-generated work as their own
  • Lack of proper attribution and citation
  • Difficulty distinguishing AI-assisted from AI-generated work
  • Erosion of original thinking and creativity
⚖️
Equity and Bias Issues
  • Unequal access to AI tools and training
  • Bias against non-native English speakers
  • Disadvantage for neurodivergent students
  • Socioeconomic disparities in AI literacy
📚
Learning & Skill Development
  • Potential loss of critical thinking skills
  • Reduced writing and research abilities
  • Over-reliance on technology for problem-solving
  • Confusion about learning objectives
🏛️
Institutional Challenges
  • Inconsistent policies across departments
  • Faculty uncertainty about enforcement
  • Resource allocation for training and tools
  • Legal and privacy considerations

Section 03
The Detection Reality
⚠️ Critical Warning

Research consistently shows that AI detection tools are unreliable and can cause significant harm through false accusations. "AI-generated text cannot be reliably detected, and simple paraphrasing is sufficient to evade detection." — University of Maryland Computer Science Study

Why AI Detection Fails
🎯
Accuracy Issues
  • 33–81% accuracy range across different tools
  • Simple paraphrasing can evade detection
  • False positives can destroy student trust
  • Tools struggle with multilingual content
⚖️
Bias Problems
  • Higher false positive rates for non-native English speakers (2–3× higher)
  • Discrimination against certain writing styles
  • Cultural and linguistic biases in training data
  • Potential legal implications under Title VI and ADA
Currently Available Tools — For Awareness Only
GPTZero

Popular among educators but shows inconsistent accuracy and bias issues.

Limitations: High false positive rates, struggles with multilingual text
Originality.AI

Claims high accuracy but independent testing shows lower performance.

Limitations: Expensive, not foolproof, can flag human writing
Turnitin AI Detection

Integrated with popular plagiarism checker but faces the same fundamental issues.

Limitations: Can't distinguish AI-assisted from AI-generated content
Better Approaches Than Detection
  • Process-Based Assessment — Track student work through multiple drafts and stages
  • In-Class Components — Include oral presentations, discussions, or demonstrations
  • Personalized Tasks — Create assignments that require unique, contextual responses
  • Transparent Expectations — Clearly communicate AI policies and expectations
  • Educational Approach — Teach students about ethical AI use rather than policing
Section 04
Building Effective AI Policies

An effective AI policy doesn't just prohibit or permit — it clarifies what learning is trying to happen, explains why the policy exists, and gives students a framework for honest disclosure.

Essential Components of an AI Policy
1. Clear Definition
Define what constitutes AI use in your context. Distinguish between AI tools and other technology. Provide specific examples of tools students might use.
2. Usage Guidelines
Specify when AI is/isn't permitted. Outline acceptable vs. unacceptable uses. Address assignment-specific variations — blanket "AI allowed" or "AI prohibited" policies are both too blunt.
3. Attribution Requirements
Explain how to cite AI-generated content (APA 7, MLA 9, and Chicago 17 all updated in 2024–2025). Require documentation of AI interactions. Include reflection on AI use when appropriate.
4. Learning Rationale
Explain why the policy exists. Connect to course learning objectives so students understand what skill they're expected to develop — and why AI use either serves or bypasses that skill.
💡 Policy Spectrum — The "Stoplight" Model

🔴 Red (AI-Free): This assignment must be completed without AI assistance — the skill being assessed requires unmediated student work.
🟡 Yellow (AI-Assisted): AI may be used for specified purposes (e.g., grammar editing, brainstorming) with required disclosure.
🟢 Green (AI-Empowered): AI use is encouraged and the assignment requires students to demonstrate critical evaluation of AI outputs.

Section 05
Authentic Assessment Strategies

The most effective response to AI in the classroom is not better detection — it's better assessment design. These five approaches make AI use either irrelevant, transparent, or part of the learning itself.

  • Process-Based Assessment — Track student work through multiple drafts and stages. AI can generate a final product easily, but not an authentic paper trail showing intellectual development over time.
  • In-Class Components — Include oral presentations, discussions, live problem-solving demonstrations, or in-class writing samples. These cannot be outsourced to AI.
  • Personalized Tasks — Create assignments that require unique, contextual responses — referencing class discussions, local events, or personal experiences AI cannot access or replicate authentically.
  • Higher-Order Cognitive Tasks — Target Analysis, Evaluation, Synthesis, and Creation (upper Bloom's levels) rather than recall or comprehension. These require judgment AI cannot fake at a professional level.
  • AI Transparency & Critique — Explicitly incorporate AI but require students to document, critique, and improve upon AI outputs. Turn the AI into a source to be verified, not a ghostwriter.
💡 The Core Principle

Focus on authentic assessment and clear policies rather than detection technology. If you design assessments that require genuine learning, the question of "did they use AI?" becomes far less urgent than "did they actually learn?"

Section 06
Scenarios in Practice
Scenario 1 · Writing Course

The situation: A student submits a polished argumentative essay. The argument is sophisticated, the prose clean — much better than her in-class writing. She says she "just had ChatGPT clean up my grammar." Your policy permits grammar/style assistance.

Framework analysis: Transparency: She told you — that matters. Purpose-fit: The harder question. If the assignment's purpose was argumentation, grammar assistance is permitted. But the gap between her in-class voice and submitted voice suggests more than grammar happened. Equity: If she's a non-native English speaker, grammar assistance is exactly what AI is best at.

Action: Don't accuse. Schedule a brief conference. Ask her to walk you through her draft history and her prompt to ChatGPT. The conversation is the assessment.

Lesson for next time: "Grammar assistance" is fuzzy. Tighten policy to specify what level of revision is in-bounds, and require submission of the original draft alongside the polished version.

Scenario 2 · STEM Lab

The situation: Two students submit nearly identical Python scripts with identical code structure and variable names. Both claim independent work.

Framework analysis: Transparency: Neither disclosed shared work or AI use. Purpose-fit: The assignment was designed to build independent coding skill — identical output is a clear signal that either collaboration or AI bypassed that skill. Attribution: If AI was used to generate boilerplate, that should have been disclosed.

Action: Interview both students separately. Ask each to walk through the logic of a specific code section. Their ability or inability to explain their own code is the evidence — far more reliable than any detector score.

Section 07
Faculty Self-Assessment & Action Plan
Preparedness Checklist
  • I have a clear, current AI policy in every syllabus — not just a general statement, but assignment-level guidance
  • I've tried 2024-or-newer versions of at least two major AI tools (GPT-5, Claude, Gemini, etc.)
  • I've had at least one explicit class conversation about AI use this semester
  • I have at least one assessment that's AI-resilient by design
  • I know what my institution's AI policy says — or that it doesn't have one
  • I know how I'd handle a suspected AI-use case without relying on a detector score
Concrete Next Steps
  • Draft an AI disclosure statement for your next assignment — just two sentences: what tool, what purpose
  • Redesign one existing assessment to include a process component (drafts, prompt logs, or oral defense)
  • Have the AI conversation with students this week — normalize disclosure before a crisis forces the issue
  • Identify your institution's AI policy status and propose filling the gap if one doesn't exist
  • Test your own assignment in ChatGPT or Claude to understand its vulnerability before your students do
