Walkthrough

Creating Illustrated Handouts with an AI Assistant

Turn a lesson outline into a print-ready, textbook-quality visual handout in five steps — with a reference table of twenty visual types and prompts you can copy and adapt.

⏱ 25 min 📑 7 sections 🎯 Walkthrough

Why this matters

Producing one good comparison chart used to mean an hour in a drawing tool or a request to whoever on campus owns the design software. Current AI image models generate a usable classroom handout from a well-organised outline in about a minute, which puts custom visuals inside the ordinary weekly prep budget for the first time.

Learning objectives

By the end of this walkthrough you will be able to:

  1. Assemble a lesson outline in the form an image model can actually work from — concepts, key terms, and named comparison dimensions.
  2. Select the visual type, from twenty, that matches what you are trying to teach.
  3. Generate a print-ready illustrated handout from one prompt, then refine it with follow-up prompts.
  4. Verify a generated visual for factual accuracy before you put it in front of students.

Prerequisites and what you will need

  • An account on an AI assistant with image generation — ChatGPT, Gemini, or Copilot. A free tier is enough, though it may limit how many images you generate per day.
  • A lesson outline you have already written. This is the real prerequisite. The walkthrough will not invent your content, and a vague outline produces a vague handout.
  • The comparison dimensions you want shown — the columns of the chart, in your own words.
  • Somewhere to put the result: a document, a slide deck, an LMS page, or a printer.
  • No design software, no drawing skill, and no image-editing knowledge.

Before step 1

What You Will Have at the End

Picture the finished artifact before you start, because it determines how you write the outline in step 1.

The finished artifact

One PNG image, roughly letter-sized in portrait, that prints legibly in black and white. Using this page's running example — a business course comparing forms of business ownership — it contains:

  • A title and subtitle across the top: “Comparison of Types of Business Ownership in the U.S.”
  • A six-column comparison chart: Sole Proprietorship, Partnership, Corporation, LLC, Cooperative, Franchise.
  • Five labelled rows: Liability, Taxes, Formation Difficulty, Ownership, Example Businesses.
  • A small illustrative icon beside each ownership type.
  • Section dividers and enough white space that a student can read it from a desk without leaning in.

It is a single flat image, not an editable document. That is the trade: you get a designed page in a minute, and you cannot fix a typo by clicking on it — you regenerate instead.

Step 1 — Prepare the Lesson Content

The one decision in this step: which comparison dimensions belong on the page. Everything downstream inherits it.

Use your detailed lesson outline about your topic, or refer to the following structure for a business class on types of business ownership:

  • Sole Proprietorship
  • Partnership
  • Corporation (C-Corp, S-Corp)
  • LLC
  • Cooperative
  • Franchise
  • Key comparison points (for example: liability, taxation, ease of formation)

Pro tip

The more specific your content outline, the better the result. List your main concepts, key terms, and the comparison dimensions you want visualised before writing your prompt.

04

Step 2 — Open the Assistant and Use the Prompt

The one decision in this step: which visual type to name in the prompt. The example below asks for a comparison chart; swap that phrase for any row from the reference table.

Use the following prompt to generate a visual handout. Copy it, then adapt it for your own subject and content:

Copy-paste prompt 
Act as an instructional designer and advanced graphic generator. Create a high-quality, visually engaging handout for students in a business class. The topic is "Comparison of Types of Business Ownership in the U.S." Generate a handout design that includes:
- A clear title and subtitle
- A visual comparison chart or infographic comparing Sole Proprietorship, Partnership, Corporation, LLC, Cooperative, and Franchise
- Key categories: Liability, Taxes, Formation Difficulty, Ownership, Example Businesses
- Clean, modern formatting suitable for print or PDF
- Business-relevant icons or illustrations to enhance visual appeal
- Use bold headings, section dividers, and enough white space for readability

Make this handout look like it belongs in a college-level business course.

Customisation tip

Replace the topic, comparison items, and categories with your own subject. The structure of this prompt works for any discipline — just swap out the content details.

05

Step 3 — Review the Generated Handout

The one decision in this step: accept, or refine. The assistant will return a photo-quality handout image, usually a PNG. Review it against these four criteria before you download anything.

  • Accuracy of the content — every label, figure and definition.
  • Clarity of layout and visual organisation — does the eye go where you want it to?
  • Proper comparison categories — the ones you asked for, spelled the way you spell them.
  • Readability — can students interpret it quickly, at desk distance?

If refinement is needed

Use follow-up prompts to improve the result:

  • “Please revise the graphic to simplify the text.”
  • “Add icons for each business type to make it more engaging.”
06

Steps 4–5 — Download, Use and Create Variations

Step 4 — download and use the graphic

  • Select the download icon on the generated image.
  • Save the file as PNG or JPEG.
  • Insert the image into Google Docs or Word handouts.
  • Use in PDF worksheets, Google Slides, or PowerPoint.
  • Embed in your LMS (Canvas, Blackboard, and so on).
  • Print for in-class distribution.

Step 5 — ask for additional variations

Use follow-up prompts to create multiple materials from the same outline:

  • “Now make a poster-sized version of the handout.”
  • “Create a worksheet version with fill-in-the-blank definitions for each business type.”
  • “Make a black-and-white version for easy printing.”
  • “Add quiz questions based on the content of the handout.”
07

Bonus Tips for Teachers

✅

Accuracy check

Always check AI-generated visuals for factual accuracy. AI can produce confident-looking graphics that contain subtle errors — review content before distributing to students.

👥

Student engagement

Consider having students interpret the handout as a warm-up or group activity — “What do you notice? What do you wonder?” works well with any visual.

📚

Cross-curricular

You can use this method to make visuals for any subject — science, history, mathematics, or English. The prompt structure works across disciplines. Just swap the content.

Verification — you are done when

Do not move on until all five of these are true. If any one fails, the fix is in the step named beside it.

  • You are holding a downloaded image file on your own machine, not a picture inside a chat window. (Step 4)
  • Every label, number and definition on it matches your source material, checked word by word. (Step 3)
  • The comparison categories are the ones you named in your outline — none silently dropped, none invented. (Step 1)
  • You printed it once, in greyscale, and could still read every cell. (Step 5, black-and-white variation)
  • You can state in one sentence what a student should understand from it that they would not get from the same text in a paragraph. (Step 1)

When it goes wrong

Troubleshooting Common Errors

Five failures account for nearly all unusable handouts. Each has a specific fix.

The text in the image is garbled or misspelled

Image models draw letterforms rather than typesetting them, so long strings degrade. Shorten every label to two or three words, ask for “minimal text, large type”, and regenerate. If a single word is still wrong, regenerate rather than trying to patch the image.

A category you asked for is missing

Long lists get truncated. Cut to a maximum of six columns and five rows, or split the content across two handouts. Naming the exact count — “exactly six columns” — helps.

The content is plausible but wrong

This is the dangerous one, because it looks finished. The model will confidently fill an empty cell with something reasonable. Check every factual claim against your own source before the handout leaves your desk; do not treat the visual polish as evidence of accuracy.

It is unreadable when printed

Screen contrast flatters. Ask for “high contrast, suitable for black-and-white printing” and generate that variation explicitly (Step 5) rather than printing the colour version.

You cannot fix the one small error

You cannot — it is a flat image. Either regenerate with a corrected prompt, or ask for the same content as a table in text form and rebuild it in a document. Deciding which is cheaper is part of the workflow.

Try this in your classroom

Take the single densest paragraph in next week's reading — the one students reliably stall on — and turn it into one visual using this workflow. Then hand it out before they read the paragraph and ask them to predict what the text will say. You get a comprehension check and a handout out of the same twenty minutes.

Key takeaways

  • The quality of the handout is set by the outline, not the prompt. Concepts, key terms and named comparison dimensions in, designed page out.
  • Twenty visual types exist because they encode different relationships. Choose by the relationship you want visible, not by which one looks best.
  • One prompt gets you a draft; follow-up prompts get you the version you use. Refining is the normal path, not a sign of failure.
  • Visual polish is generated independently of factual accuracy, so a handsome chart proves nothing. Verify every claim before it reaches students.
