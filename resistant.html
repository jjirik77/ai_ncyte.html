Critical Brief

Creating AI-Resistant Assignments

Strategies for designing authentic and effective assessments in the age of generative AI. The goal is not to make assignments "AI-proof" — but to design assessments where AI use provides limited benefit or must be transparent and critically evaluated.

⏱ 25 min 📑 4 sections 🎯 Critical Brief

Why this matters

"AI-proof" is not achievable, and chasing it burns a term. What is achievable is an assignment where AI gives little advantage, or where its use has to be visible. That is a design problem rather than an enforcement problem — and it is solved with layers, never with one clever method.

Learning objectives

By the end of this resource you will be able to:

  1. Evaluate an existing assignment for AI substitutability and judge which of its requirements AI already satisfies.
  2. Defend a chosen combination of methods — rather than a single method — for one specific assignment in your discipline.
  3. Distinguish methods that reduce AI's benefit from methods that make AI use visible, and say which kind a given assignment needs.
  4. Judge a redesign against its learning outcome rather than against AI-proofing, and decide when AI-assisted completion is acceptable.

01

Welcome & Core Philosophy

Welcome, Educators! The goal is not to make assignments "AI-proof" (which is likely impossible), but to design assessments where using AI provides limited benefit or where AI use must be transparent and critically evaluated. The most effective approach combines multiple strategies.

💡 The Core Principle

Think of these methods as a toolkit, not a checklist. A single method rarely makes an assignment AI-resistant by itself. The power comes from combining approaches — for example, pairing a personalized task (Method 1) with a process journal (Method 2) and an in-class oral component (Method 6). Each layer makes AI substitution less useful and genuine engagement more visible.

Why This Matters

  • AI can now generate plausible first drafts, essays, and code within seconds
  • Detection tools are unreliable and can harm innocent students — especially non-native English speakers
  • Well-designed assignments reduce the benefit of AI substitution without requiring surveillance
  • Students who engage authentically develop skills that AI-reliant peers don't — a long-term advantage
02

Personalization, Process, and Authentic Work

Method 1

Personalization and Specificity

Assignments must require students to draw on unique personal experiences, specific contexts, or localized information that AI cannot access or replicate authentically.

Example (Cybersecurity): Ask students to assess their own home network security and document specific vulnerabilities found, rather than a generic network.

Method 2

Process-Based Assessment (Scaffolding)

Break large assignments into smaller, interconnected components that track student thinking and development over time. AI can generate a final product easily, but not the authentic paper trail.

Example (AI/Programming): Require commit history, code evolution documentation, and explanation of debugging processes for every coding project.

Method 3

Metacognitive and Reflective Components

Require students to explain their thinking process, justify their choices, and reflect on their learning journey. AI can produce an answer, but not an authentic reflection on how a student arrived at it.

Example: Explain why certain approaches were chosen over alternatives (e.g., why a specific algorithm was selected over alternatives).

Method 4

Authentic Assessment

Create assignments that mirror real-world professional tasks and require application of knowledge in complex, context-rich situations rather than simple recall.

Example (Cybersecurity): Conduct actual penetration testing on authorized systems (like a sandbox environment) with detailed reporting, mimicking a real industry job.

Method 5

Multimodal Assessment

Use non-traditional formats that require the integration of multiple skills and media types that AI currently struggles to produce cohesively and uniquely.

Example (Networking): Create video demonstrations of configuration processes with verbal explanations and visual proof of concept.

Method 6

Synchronous and In-Person Components

Incorporate real-time, face-to-face elements that cannot be outsourced to AI.

Examples: Oral defenses, live problem-solving demonstrations, in-class writing samples, or "Viva voce" oral examinations.

Method 7

Collaborative and Social Learning Tasks

Assignments requiring genuine human interaction, negotiation, and group dynamics. AI can write an essay, but it can't participate in group dynamics.

Examples: Project-based learning with real-world deliverables, or peer review with substantive feedback requirements.

03

Complexity, AI Transparency, and Experience

Method 8

Higher-Order Cognitive Tasks (Bloom's Taxonomy)

Design assignments that target Analysis, Evaluation, Synthesis, and Creation (upper levels) rather than lower levels (Remember, Understand, Apply).

Example (Security): Evaluate the effectiveness of different security frameworks (e.g., NIST vs. ISO 27001) for a specific organizational context and justify the choice.

Method 9

AI Transparency and Critical Engagement

Explicitly incorporate AI but require students to document, critique, and improve upon AI outputs. Turn the AI into a partner or a source to be verified.

Example: Have students input assignment prompts into multiple AI tools, evaluate and compare responses, and then provide a heavily modified final version with documentation of AI errors and biases.

Method 10

Context-Specific Case Studies

Use detailed, specific scenarios with multiple variables that require deep analysis rather than generic responses that AI can easily pull from its training data.

Example (Networking): Troubleshoot specific network diagrams with unique configurations and symptoms that are not publicly documented.

Method 11

Iterative Revision with Instructor Feedback

Assignments built around multiple drafts with personalized instructor feedback that students must incorporate and explain. The personalization of the feedback makes the next revision nearly impossible for a general-purpose AI to complete authentically.

Key: Personalized feedback creates a paper trail of intellectual development that AI cannot fabricate.

Method 12

Time-Bound Assessment

Assessments completed within limited timeframes that make extensive AI consultation impractical or impossible to integrate seamlessly.

Examples: Live coding challenges, in-class essays, or rapid response discussions where the timing itself limits AI use.

Method 13

Experiential and Field-Based Learning

Assignments grounded in direct experience outside the classroom that AI cannot access or fake convincingly.

Example (IT): Site visits to data centers or local businesses with required photographic/video documentation and security assessment reports based on firsthand observation.

04

Best Practices & Implementation

Clear Communication

  • Establish transparent AI use policies (e.g., "stoplight" model: AI-free/red, AI-assisted/yellow, AI-empowered/green)
  • Develop detailed rubrics that emphasize criteria AI cannot meet well (e.g., originality, personal reflection, process documentation)
  • Explain the why behind each policy — students who understand the learning purpose are more likely to comply authentically

Assessment Literacy

  • Test your own assignments with AI tools to understand their vulnerabilities before your students do
  • Teach students about AI capabilities and limitations as part of critical literacy — not as a warning, but as curriculum
  • Revisit assessments annually as AI capabilities evolve rapidly

Focus on Learning Outcomes

  • Always ask: "What skill is this assignment designed to develop?" before redesigning it
  • The goal is not AI-proofing — it's ensuring that the assignment requires the learning it's designed to produce
  • If a student can complete the assignment with AI and genuinely learn from the process, that may be acceptable
  • If a student can complete the assignment with AI and bypass the intended learning, redesign is needed

💡 Final Reminder

The most effective approach combines multiple strategies from this list. No single method is foolproof, but combining personalization + process documentation + an in-class component creates layers of accountability that make authentic engagement far more visible than AI substitution.

Judgment check

You add a 500-word reflection to a research paper, asking students to explain how they chose their sources. The reflections come back fluent, sincere, and completely generic. What went wrong?

Method 3 only bites when it is anchored to artifacts that exist. Pair it with Method 2: require the reflection to reference the annotated search log, the source that was rejected, or your feedback on draft 2 — specifics that only exist for a student who did the work, and that the AI never saw.
The method is not weak and the instruction is not the gap — the anchor is. A reflection on an untracked process can only ask for generic process talk, which is precisely what AI produces fluently. Pair Method 3 with Method 2 (scaffolding): require the reflection to cite the annotated search log, the rejected source, or your feedback on draft 2 — specifics that exist only for a student who did the work.

Try this in your classroom

Take one assignment and add exactly two layers from different families: one that lowers AI's payoff (personalize it to your campus, your lab, or this semester's case) and one that makes use visible (a draft, a prompt log, or a two-minute defense). Two layers on one assignment is the realistic ask for a single term.

Group discussion

Method 12 (time-bound) and Method 6 (synchronous) are among the most AI-resistant methods and among the least accessible. Spend five minutes on it: which students in your program do they disadvantage, and what accommodation keeps the method without the harm?

Concrete next steps

Five actions you can finish this month:

  1. Paste your most AI-vulnerable assignment verbatim into an AI assistant this week and save the output as a baseline.
  2. Rewrite one prompt so it requires this campus, this semester, or this student's own data (Method 1).
  3. Split one large assignment into two graded checkpoints with a required interim artifact (Method 2).
  4. Add a two-minute oral defense to one assignment — and time it against your actual class size before committing (Method 6).
  5. Write the stoplight label — red, yellow, or green — beside every assignment on your syllabus.

Key takeaways

  • Run the assignment through an AI assistant first: whatever it produces well is the part your requirements are not actually protecting.
  • No single method resists AI. Personalization plus a process trail plus one synchronous component is what creates layered accountability.
  • Some methods lower AI's payoff and others raise its visibility — a strong redesign takes one of each rather than two of the same kind.
  • The test is the learning outcome, not the AI: if a student can use AI and still do the learning, that is a pass; if they can use AI and skip it, redesign.
