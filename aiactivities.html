Building Interactive Learning Content

A six-stage AI-powered instructional design framework for turning traditional course materials into dynamic, engaging, and accessible learning experiences — from the learning outcomes you write first to the eight deliverables you leave with.

⏱ 5–7 hours 📑 8 sections 🎯 Faculty Workshop 📚 15 key terms Reviewed August 2026

Why this matters

Most faculty meet AI at the wrong end of the process — asking it for content before deciding what students must be able to do. The result looks finished and teaches nothing in particular. This workshop puts outcomes first and treats AI as a drafting partner you direct, verify, and refine.

Learning objectives

By the end of this workshop you will be able to:

  1. Design a learning-experience specification — outcomes, Bloom’s levels, prior knowledge, misconceptions, and assessment — before generating any content
  2. Produce the course architecture (outline, syllabus, lesson plans, scope and sequence) with AI drafting and you verifying
  3. Create instructional content — lecture segments, study guides, examples, and discussion prompts — that you approve as the subject-matter expert
  4. Design interactive learning assets (flashcards, scenarios, escape rooms, games, simulations, chatbots) that move students up the ICAP ladder
  5. Produce digital and multimedia resources using the right AI tool for each content type
  6. Refine AI-generated materials against accuracy, alignment, accessibility, and engagement until they are ready to publish
  7. Construct reusable CO-STAR prompts that generate aligned instructional content at any stage

Stage 1: Define the Learning Experience

Foundation · ~40 Minutes

Analogy: Defining the learning experience is like filing a flight plan before takeoff. Pilots don't start the engines and then decide where they're going. Before generating a single piece of content, you must know your destination (outcomes), your passengers (learners), and how you'll know you've arrived (assessment). Without this, AI produces impressive-looking content that goes nowhere meaningful.

Why This Stage Comes First

Research confirms that AI-generated educational content frequently fails pedagogically not because it lacks fluency, but because it prioritizes coherence over meaningful learner engagement. The antidote is clear instructional intent before AI generation begins. Stage 1 creates the specifications that all subsequent AI outputs must satisfy.

Structure Before Content

AI can generate complete structural frameworks in minutes, giving instructors a structured starting point to refine rather than a blank page to fill.

Stage 3: Generate Instructional Content

Content Creation · ~50 Minutes

Analogy: Stage 3 is filling the rooms you designed in Stage 2. AI acts like a highly knowledgeable content writer who works overnight — producing a first draft of every room's furnishings. Your role shifts from creator to editor: you curate, verify, and refine rather than generate from scratch.

AI as Drafting Partner, Faculty as Expert Editor

AI can dramatically accelerate content creation — generating a lecture segment in seconds that might take an instructor an hour to write. This doesn't replace faculty expertise; it redirects it toward higher-value work: verifying accuracy, adding disciplinary nuance, and ensuring student relevance. Faculty remain the subject matter experts who review, curate, and approve.

Stage 4: Create Interactive Learning Assets

Activation · ~60 Minutes

Research Finding: The ICAP framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014) demonstrates that learning outcomes systematically improve as learners move from passive (listening) to active (doing) to constructive (creating) to interactive (debating). AI now makes it feasible to build interactive assets — scenarios, simulations, escape rooms — in the same time it previously took to write a lecture slide deck.

Why Interactivity Is Non-Negotiable

Studies consistently show that interactive and hands-on learning environments outperform passive instructional formats in comprehension, motivation, and long-term retention. Yet most AI-generated content defaults to passive formats — text explanations and bullet lists. Stage 4 is where you deliberately break that pattern.

CO-STAR for Instructional Design

Prompt Framework · ~30 Minutes

Where this framework is taught

CO-STAR is the workshop’s single prompt framework, taught in full in Module 3 — AI Prompts & Prompt Engineering, which carries the component-by-component explanation, the worked refinements, and its own builder. This section does not re-teach it. It shows what the same six components look like when the thing you are generating is instructional content, where Objective means a learning outcome and Response means a deliverable you can put in front of students.

Purpose: CO-STAR gives educators a consistent, six-component structure for generating high-quality instructional content at any of the six stages. It’s the instructional design equivalent of a recipe card — every component has a purpose, and missing one degrades the final result.

The Six Components, Read as an Instructional Designer

C

Context

What course and learning environment is this for, and what have students already done? Provide the background the AI cannot infer.

"This is the fourth week of a blended first-year course; students have completed the prerequisite lab but have no field experience."

O

Objective

What must the material accomplish? State it as the learning outcome, with the Bloom’s level, plus any bound on scope the output must respect.

"Produce materials that get students to ANALYZE (Bloom’s L4) competing economic theories using evidence. Nothing requiring off-campus fieldwork."

S

Style

What kind of writing, and from whose expertise? This is where the professional identity you would once have called a "role" belongs.

"Write as an experienced instructional designer and subject matter expert in [discipline]. Reading level: Grade 10."

T

Tone

What attitude should the voice carry toward these learners? Left unset, AI defaults to a flat neutral-professional register that reads as impersonal to novices.

"Encouraging and concrete — these students are new to the discipline and easily discouraged by jargon."

A

Audience

Who is the material for? Name the learners, their level, and their prior knowledge. This drives vocabulary, depth, and which examples land.

"First-year community college students, most taking their first course in the discipline."

R

Response

What artifacts should AI produce, in what shape, meeting what requirements? Be specific about the number of components, the format, and accessibility.

"A lesson plan in table format, 20 flashcards, one scenario activity, one exit ticket. Include UDL considerations and alt text for any diagram. Cap activities at 30 minutes."

CO-STAR in Action: Complete Example

Full CO-STAR Prompt Example[CONTEXT] This is a hybrid ADN nursing program. The lesson sits in the term before students begin clinical rotations, and the college requires that every activity be completable on campus. [OBJECTIVE] Produce a lesson package that gets students to APPLY (Bloom's L3) basic patient assessment protocols and ANALYZE (Bloom's L4) vital sign data to identify clinical concerns. Nothing in it may depend on clinical placement. [STYLE] Write as an experienced instructional designer and subject matter expert in healthcare education. Reading level: Grade 10. [TONE] Encouraging and clinically precise — these are first-year students meeting professional standards for the first time. [AUDIENCE] First-year nursing students who have completed Anatomy I and have no clinical experience yet. [RESPONSE] Create: (1) a 45-minute lesson plan in table format, (2) a patient assessment simulation scenario, (3) 15 flashcards on vital sign normal ranges, and (4) a formative exit ticket with 3 questions. Include alt text descriptions for any referenced diagrams.

Two components that move when you write for instruction

Notice where the professional identity and the requirements ended up. "Act as an instructional designer" is a Style instruction, not background — it tells the AI how to write, not what situation it is in. And a list of requirements splits: limits on the artifact (reading level, alt text, table format, 30-minute cap) belong to Response, while limits on scope ("nothing requiring clinical placement") belong to Objective, because they define what counts as having succeeded.

Key takeaways

  • Begin with learning outcomes — every AI generation starts here, not with a content idea. Outcomes are the specification the output must satisfy.
  • Build the architecture before filling it: an outline, syllabus, and scope-and-sequence generated in Stage 2 save hours of reorganization later.
  • AI accelerates content creation; faculty expertise supplies the accuracy, disciplinary nuance, and student fit that make it usable.
  • Interactive assets are not extras. The ICAP framework places interactive engagement above active and passive, and Stage 4 is where you deliberately break AI’s default toward passive text.
  • Digital and multimedia production is now open to all faculty regardless of technical background — the skill is matching the tool to the content type.
  • Evaluation across accuracy, alignment, accessibility, and engagement is not a final check but a cycle that feeds back into the earlier stages.
  • CO-STAR gives you one reusable prompt structure — Context, Objective, Style, Tone, Audience, Response — for every piece of instructional content you will ever generate. It is the same framework Module 3 teaches; here Objective is a learning outcome and Response is a deliverable.
