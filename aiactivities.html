📖 Key Terms Glossary

👆 Hover or click a card to flip it and reveal the definition

Artificial Intelligence (AI)

What role does it play in content creation?

🔄 Flip
Definition

Computer systems that emulate human cognitive functions — reasoning, problem-solving, and learning — to automate and accelerate the creation of instructional materials.

Generative AI

How is this different from traditional AI?

🔄 Flip
Definition

AI that creates new content — text, images, audio, video, and code — from patterns learned during training. The engine behind AI-generated lesson plans, quizzes, and interactive tools.

Learning Outcomes

Why must these come first?

🔄 Flip
Definition

Specific, measurable statements describing what students will know or be able to do after instruction. All content, activities, and assessments must trace back to them.

Instructional Design

What is the systematic approach?

🔄 Flip
Definition

The systematic process of creating effective educational experiences — analyzing learner needs, designing objectives, developing content, and evaluating outcomes. AI dramatically accelerates each phase.

Course Alignment

What must align with what?

🔄 Flip
Definition

The coherence between learning outcomes, instructional activities, and assessments. A well-aligned course ensures every element — from lectures to exams — works toward the same goals.

Prompt Engineering

Why is this an instructional skill?

🔄 Flip
Definition

The deliberate design of AI instructions to produce high-quality, relevant educational outputs. For instructional designers, it's the bridge between pedagogy and AI capability.

Interactive Learning

What does research say about it?

🔄 Flip
Definition

Learning experiences requiring active student participation — not passive reception. The ICAP framework confirms interactive engagement consistently produces stronger outcomes than passive formats.

Scaffolding

How does this support learners?

🔄 Flip
Definition

Temporary instructional support that gradually decreases as learners build competence. Like construction scaffolding — essential during building, removed once the structure stands on its own.

Formative Assessment

How does it differ from grading?

🔄 Flip
Definition

Ongoing assessment designed to monitor learning and provide feedback during instruction — not to assign a final grade. Exit tickets, quizzes, and polls are formative. Final exams are summative.

Gamification

Is this just about making things "fun"?

🔄 Flip
Definition

Applying game mechanics (points, leaderboards, challenges, badges) to non-game educational contexts to increase motivation and engagement. Research confirms it boosts both enjoyment and retention.

Learning Objects

What makes something a learning object?

🔄 Flip
Definition

Self-contained, reusable instructional units with a defined learning objective — a flashcard set, simulation, video, or quiz. AI can generate learning objects at scale, which human designers then assemble and quality-check.

Microlearning

Why does bite-size matter?

🔄 Flip
Definition

Delivering content in short, focused segments (3–7 minutes) targeting a single objective. Aligns with how working memory processes new information — small bites reduce cognitive overload.

Accessibility

Who benefits from accessible design?

🔄 Flip
Definition

Designing instructional materials usable by all learners — including those with disabilities, ESL learners, and diverse learning needs. Accessible design benefits every learner, not just those with documented needs.

Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

What are its three principles?

🔄 Flip
Definition

A framework providing multiple means of: (1) Representation — how content is presented; (2) Action & Expression — how students demonstrate learning; (3) Engagement — how to motivate diverse learners.

Iterative Development

Why is the first draft not the final product?

🔄 Flip
Definition

A cyclical design approach: Draft → Test → Evaluate → Refine → Repeat. AI-generated content requires multiple revision cycles to reach academic quality — iteration is not failure; it's the process.

Stage 1: Define the Learning Experience
Foundation · Begin Here Before Generating Anything
Analogy: Defining the learning experience is like filing a flight plan before takeoff. Pilots don't start the engines and then decide where they're going. Before generating a single piece of content, you must know your destination (outcomes), your passengers (learners), and how you'll know you've arrived (assessment). Without this, AI produces impressive-looking content that goes nowhere meaningful.

Why This Stage Comes First

Research confirms that AI-generated educational content frequently fails pedagogically not because it lacks fluency, but because it prioritizes coherence over meaningful learner engagement. The antidote is clear instructional intent before AI generation begins. Stage 1 creates the specifications that all subsequent AI outputs must satisfy.

Six Questions to Answer Before Generating Content

👆 Click each card to see why each question is critical:

What course or lesson am I creating?

Scope and subject

🔄 Flip
Why it matters

Without a defined scope, AI generates "everything" — producing unfocused, generically organized content that instructors then spend hours trimming. Scope first, generate second.

What are the learning outcomes?

Measurable destination

🔄 Flip
Why it matters

Outcomes are the anchor for every AI prompt. When you include them in prompts, AI generates aligned content rather than general-purpose material that may not serve your course.

What Bloom's levels are involved?

Cognitive demand

🔄 Flip
Why it matters

AI defaults to lower Bloom's levels (Remember, Understand) unless told otherwise. Specifying "Analyze" or "Evaluate" in your prompt produces dramatically different and more rigorous content.

What prior knowledge is required?

Entry point

🔄 Flip
Why it matters

Telling AI what students already know prevents over-explanation of basics and under-explanation of new material. It calibrates the starting point of every generated resource.

What misconceptions should be addressed?

Predictive error correction

🔄 Flip
Why it matters

Instructors who know their students' common misconceptions can prompt AI to address them directly — producing materials that proactively correct errors rather than just presenting correct information.

How will learning be assessed?

Backward design

🔄 Flip
Why it matters

Backward design (Wiggins & McTighe) starts with the assessment, then designs instruction. Knowing the assessment upfront allows AI to generate aligned learning experiences — not just content that might relate.

Stage 3: Generate Instructional Content
Content Creation · Accelerate Without Sacrificing Quality
Analogy: Stage 3 is filling the rooms you designed in Stage 2. AI acts like a highly knowledgeable content writer who works overnight — producing a first draft of every room's furnishings. Your role shifts from creator to editor: you curate, verify, and refine rather than generate from scratch.

AI as Drafting Partner, Faculty as Expert Editor

AI can dramatically accelerate content creation — generating a lecture segment in seconds that might take an instructor an hour to write. This doesn't replace faculty expertise; it redirects it toward higher-value work: verifying accuracy, adding disciplinary nuance, and ensuring student relevance. Faculty remain the subject matter experts who review, curate, and approve.

Stage 4: Create Interactive Learning Assets
Activation · Transform Passive Into Active
Research Finding: The ICAP framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014) demonstrates that learning outcomes systematically improve as learners move from passive (listening) to active (doing) to constructive (creating) to interactive (debating). AI now makes it feasible to build interactive assets — scenarios, simulations, escape rooms — in the same time it previously took to write a lecture slide deck.

Why Interactivity Is Non-Negotiable

Studies consistently show that interactive and hands-on learning environments outperform passive instructional formats in comprehension, motivation, and long-term retention. Yet most AI-generated content defaults to passive formats — text explanations and bullet lists. Stage 4 is where you deliberately break that pattern.

Stage 6: Evaluate, Improve & Personalize
Quality Assurance · First Draft Is Never the Final Product
Analogy: Stage 6 is the home inspection before you move in — and the ongoing maintenance after. No responsible homeowner skips the inspection. No responsible educator publishes AI-generated content without review. Iterative development is not a sign of weakness; it is the design process itself.

The Four Evaluation Dimensions

Every piece of AI-generated instructional content must be reviewed across four critical dimensions before deployment. AI can assist with all four reviews — but faculty judgment remains irreplaceable for disciplinary accuracy and student fit.

👆 Click each dimension card to see what to check:

🎯 Accuracy

Is the content factually correct and bias-free?

🔄 Review checklist
Check

Fact-check all claims — AI hallucinates. Verify sources independently. Screen for disciplinary bias. Confirm statistics are current. Check that examples are culturally appropriate for your student population.

📌 Alignment

Does every element connect to a learning outcome?

🔄 Review checklist
Check

Trace every activity and assessment back to a specific outcome. If you can't connect a content piece to an outcome, either add the outcome or remove the piece. Misalignment is the most common AI content flaw.

♿ Accessibility

Can all students access and use this content?

🔄 Review checklist
Check

Screen reader compatibility, alt text for images, high-contrast colors, captions for audio/video, appropriate reading level (specify in your prompt), and keyboard navigation for interactive elements.

🔥 Engagement

Will students actually interact with this?

🔄 Review checklist
Check

Count the interaction opportunities per 10 minutes of content. Review for student interest triggers — relevance, novelty, challenge level, choice. Passive content fails engagement regardless of AI quality.

The RCODC Prompt Framework
Structured AI Prompting for Instructional Content Creation
Purpose: The RCODC framework gives educators a consistent, five-component structure for generating high-quality instructional content at any of the six stages. It's the instructional design equivalent of a recipe card — every component has a purpose, and missing one degrades the final result.

The Five Components

R

Role

Who should the AI become? Assign a professional identity with domain expertise.

"Act as an experienced instructional designer and subject matter expert in [discipline]."
C

Context

What course, audience, and learning environment? Provide the background the AI needs.

"Create materials for first-year community college students in a blended learning environment."
O

Objectives

What should students learn? Include Bloom's level, specific outcomes, and prior knowledge.

"Students will analyze, compare, and evaluate competing economic theories using evidence."
D

Deliverable

What should AI produce? Be specific about the format and number of components.

"Create a lesson plan, 20 flashcards, one scenario activity, and one exit ticket."
C

Constraints

What requirements must the output meet? Bloom's level, accessibility, length, format.

"Use Bloom's levels 3–5. Include UDL considerations. Limit activities to 30 minutes each."

RCODC in Action: Complete Example

Full RCODC Prompt Example
[ROLE] Act as an experienced instructional designer and subject matter expert in healthcare education. [CONTEXT] Create materials for first-year nursing students (ADN program) in a hybrid learning environment. Students have completed Anatomy I but have no clinical experience yet. [OBJECTIVES] Students will APPLY (Bloom's L3) basic patient assessment protocols and ANALYZE (Bloom's L4) vital sign data to identify clinical concerns. [DELIVERABLE] Create: (1) a 45-minute lesson plan, (2) a patient assessment simulation scenario, (3) 15 flashcards on vital sign normal ranges, and (4) a formative exit ticket with 3 questions. [CONSTRAINTS] All activities must be completable without clinical placement. Include alt text descriptions for any referenced diagrams. Reading level: Grade 10. Present lesson plan in table format.

Key Takeaways

  • Begin with learning outcomes — all AI generation must start here, not with content ideas
  • Build the architecture before filling it — Stage 2 saves hours of reorganization later
  • AI accelerates content creation; faculty expertise ensures its accuracy and relevance
  • Interactive assets (Stage 4) are not extras — they are the primary drivers of student engagement and retention
  • Digital production (Stage 5) is now accessible to all faculty regardless of technical background
  • Stage 6 evaluation is not optional — it is the quality control that makes AI-generated content academically credible
  • The RCODC framework gives you a reusable prompt structure for every piece of instructional content you'll ever create
  • Iterative development means the first AI draft is always a starting point, never a finished product
