Concept Lesson

Understanding LLM Tokenization

A practical guide to tokens, context windows, and what AI actually costs — with live calculators you can try on your own text.

⏱ 30 min 📑 5 sections 🎯 Concept Lesson

Why this matters

Assistants bill, limit, and forget in tokens — not in words. Once you can see the token, three opaque behaviours become predictable: why a long document gets truncated, why the model loses the start of a conversation, and why one task costs a fraction of a cent and another costs a dollar.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. Define a token and explain how an assistant splits ordinary text into whole words, subwords, punctuation, and special characters.
  2. Explain what a context window is and compare the limits of current models in equivalent pages of text.
  3. Estimate the cost of a task from its input and output token counts, and explain why output is billed at a higher rate than input.
  4. Describe four behaviours that change the token bill — multilingual tokenization, prompt caching, batch processing, and reasoning tokens.
  5. Compare models for a specific teaching, research, or administrative task and name a strategy that lowers its token cost.

01

What Are Tokens?

Tokens are the fundamental building blocks that Large Language Models use to understand and process text. Think of them as the "words" in the AI's vocabulary, though they're more sophisticated than simple words.

📚 Library Analogy

Imagine a vast library where each book represents a piece of text you want to process. Tokens are like the individual pages of these books. Just as a librarian processes a book page by page to understand its content, an LLM processes text token by token to understand meaning and generate responses.

Token Types

Whole Words
"hello", "world" — common short words often map to a single token.
Subwords
"un-", "ing", "-tion" — common prefixes, suffixes, and word stems.
Punctuation
".", "!", "?" — punctuation marks each typically form their own token.
Special Characters
"@", "#", numbers — symbols and digits may each become separate tokens.

🔧 Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) Explained

Think of BPE like a smart text compressor. It starts with individual characters and gradually builds up common patterns. For example, if "ing" appears frequently, it becomes a single token instead of three separate letters. This helps the AI understand common word patterns more efficiently.

02

Context Windows

The context window is like the AI's "working memory" — it determines how much information the model can consider at once when generating responses.

🧠 Human Memory Analogy

Imagine you're having a conversation with a friend. Your brain can remember the last few minutes of conversation clearly, but details from an hour ago might be fuzzy. Similarly, an LLM's context window determines how much of your conversation it can "remember" and use to generate relevant responses.

Quick check

A colleague says a model with a 1,000,000-token context window has “unlimited memory.” What is the most accurate correction?

Correct. The context window is working memory for the whole exchange, not an inbox for your input. A long conversation fills it the same way one long document does, and once it is full the earliest content stops being available — which is exactly what “the model forgot what I said at the start” means.
Not quite. The context window is finite and shared: your documents, your questions, and the model’s own replies all draw on the same budget. When it fills, the earliest content drops out — which is what people are describing when they say the model “forgot” the beginning of a conversation.
03

Pricing & Costs

Understanding LLM pricing is crucial for anyone using these tools professionally or educationally. Most providers use a token-based pricing model with separate rates for input and output.

⚡ Electricity Bill Analogy

Think of LLM pricing like your electricity bill. Just as you pay for the electricity you consume (measured in kilowatt-hours), you pay for the "computational power" you use (measured in tokens). Input tokens are like the power needed to turn on your appliances, while output tokens are like the power consumed while they're running — typically higher because generation requires more computational work.

💡 Why Output Costs More

Think of input processing like reading a book (the AI just needs to understand what you're saying), while output generation is like writing a book (the AI must create new, coherent content word by word). Writing requires much more mental effort and computational resources than reading, which is why output tokens cost 2–5× more than input tokens.

04

Advanced Concepts

Dive deeper into the technical aspects of tokenization and how they impact real-world applications.

🌍
Multilingual Tokenization
Different languages have vastly different tokenization efficiency. Chinese text might use 1.5× more tokens than English for the same meaning, while some languages like German can be even more token-intensive due to compound words.
🔄
Prompt Caching
Many providers now offer "prompt caching" — if you repeatedly use the same system prompt or context, they'll cache it and give you up to 90% discounts on those cached tokens.
⚡
Batch Processing
For non-urgent tasks, batch processing can provide 50% discounts by processing multiple requests together, though with slower response times.
🧠
Reasoning Tokens
Reasoning models think before they answer, using hidden "thinking" tokens that are billed as output. On current Claude models this is on by default, so a short answer can still carry a large output-token bill.

🏭 The Token Factory

Imagine a factory that processes raw materials (text) into finished products (AI responses). The tokenizer is like the factory's intake system — it breaks down incoming materials into standard units that the factory machines can process. Different factories (models) might prefer different unit sizes, which is why the same text gets tokenized differently across models.

🎪 The AI Talent Show

Think of different AI models like performers in a talent show. The frontier models are the expensive headliners who can carry a complex act; the mid-tier models are the reliable all-rounders; and the small, fast models are the energetic opening act who warm up the crowd for a fraction of the fee. Choose the right performer for your show (task) rather than always booking the headliner.

Quick check

You plan to send a 1,000-word prompt and expect a 500-word answer back. What is the more reliable way to predict what that exchange costs?

Correct. Billing is per token, not per word, and the two directions carry different rates — output typically costs two to five times more than input, because generating text is more computational work than reading it. Pricing the two halves separately is what makes the estimate hold up.
Not quite. There is no per-word price, and the answer is billed to you as output — usually at two to five times the input rate. Convert both halves to tokens (about four characters, or 0.75 words, each) and price them separately.

Key takeaways

  • A token is not a word. Text is split into whole words, subwords, punctuation, and special characters — roughly four characters, or 0.75 English words, each. That unit is what everything else is measured in.
  • The context window is shared working memory. Your documents, your questions, and the model’s own replies all draw on the same budget, and its size is best understood in pages of text rather than raw token counts.
  • Cost follows tokens in both directions. Estimate input and output separately, and expect output to be billed higher — generating text is more work than reading it.
  • Four things move the bill. Non-English text tokenizes less efficiently, prompt caching and batch processing discount repeat and non-urgent work, and reasoning models spend hidden thinking tokens that land in your output count.
  • Match the model to the task. The smallest model that can do the job, plus a tighter prompt, is usually a larger saving than any negotiation over price.
